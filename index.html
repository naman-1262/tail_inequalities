01 — The Three Inequalities

From Weak to Exponentially Strong

Tail inequalities answer a fundamental question: how likely is a random variable to deviate far from its mean? Each inequality trades assumptions for tightness — the more you know about your distribution, the stronger the bound you can claim.

📐

Markov's Inequality

Weakest · Most General

The simplest tail bound. For any non-negative random variable X with finite mean, it upper-bounds the probability that X exceeds any positive threshold a. Requires almost nothing about the distribution.

P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X] / a

REQUIREMENTS

  • X must be non-negative
  • Finite expectation E[X] must exist
  • No other distributional assumptions
📊

Chebyshev's Inequality

Moderate · Uses Variance

Derived by applying Markov to the squared deviation (X − μ)². Bounds the probability of deviating more than k standard deviations from the mean in either direction. Works for any distribution with finite variance.

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²

REQUIREMENTS

  • Finite mean μ
  • Finite variance σ²
  • Any distribution shape allowed
🚀

Chernoff Bound

Strongest · Exponential Decay

Uses moment generating functions to deliver exponentially decaying bounds. Specifically powerful for sums of independent variables. The deviation parameter δ controls how far above the mean you are asking about.

P(X ≥ (1+δ)μ) ≤ e^(−μδ²/3)

REQUIREMENTS

  • Sum of independent variables
  • Bounded or Bernoulli variables
  • Moment generating function must exist
02 — When & Why

Intuition & Applications

Markov — The Universal Safety Net

Markov is your last resort — use it when you know nothing about the distribution except the mean. The proof is elegant: since E[X] aggregates all of the probability mass, any mass sitting above threshold a must be bounded by E[X]/a.

  • Gives polynomial (1/a) decay — the weakest possible
  • Always valid; never requires more than E[X]
  • Foundation from which Chebyshev is derived
  • Used when only the mean is known
Example: Average exam score = 70. Markov says at most 70% of students scored ≥ 100. Trivially true but often the best possible statement without more information.

Chebyshev — The Variance-Aware Bound

Apply Markov to Y = (X − μ)² ≥ 0. This squared trick lets variance enter the picture: P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) = P((X−μ)² ≥ k²σ²) ≤ σ²/(k²σ²) = 1/k². The decay is now quadratic — significantly sharper than Markov.

  • Works for any distribution with finite variance
  • 1/k² decay — much better than Markov's 1/k
  • Key tool in proving the Weak Law of Large Numbers
  • Great for convergence and streaming analysis
Example: μ=50, σ=10. P(|X−50| ≥ 30) ≤ 1/9 ≈ 11.1%, regardless of whether the distribution is normal, uniform, or anything else.

Chernoff — The Exponential Powerhouse

Apply Markov to the MGF: P(X ≥ a) = P(e^tX ≥ e^ta) ≤ E[e^tX]/e^ta. Optimize over t to find the tightest possible bound. For sums of independent Bernoullis, this collapses to the clean exponential form. The parameter δ = (a − μ)/μ measures relative deviation.

  • Requires independence — a crucial assumption
  • Exponential e^(−δ²μ/3) decay vs Chebyshev's 1/k²
  • Essential in randomized algorithms and hashing
  • Foundation of PAC learning sample complexity bounds
Example: Flip 1000 fair coins (μ=500). P(≥600 heads) with δ=0.2: Chernoff gives e^(−500·0.04/3) ≈ e^(−6.67) ≈ 0.0013%. Chebyshev gives 2.5% — Chernoff is 2000× tighter here.

The Hierarchy at a Glance

Think of the three as a ladder of assumptions vs. strength:

  • Markov — only needs E[X] ≥ 0; bound decays as 1/a
  • Chebyshev — needs mean + variance; bound decays as 1/k²
  • Chernoff — needs independence; bound decays as e^(−δ²μ)
Every step up the ladder costs one additional assumption but rewards you with dramatically tighter tail control. In algorithm analysis, the exponential gap between Chebyshev and Chernoff often means the difference between a useful bound and a useless one.

04 — Side by Side

Which One Is Better?

Each inequality occupies a different niche. "Better" depends entirely on what you know about your random variable.

Property Markov Chebyshev Chernoff
Assumptions needed Non-negative, E[X] finite Finite mean + variance Independent variables, MGF exists
Tail decay rate 1/a  (polynomial) 1/k² (polynomial) e^(−δ²μ/3) (exponential)
Bound tightness Weakest Moderate Strongest
Distribution agnostic ✓ Fully ✓ Fully ✗ Needs independence
One-sided / two-sided One-sided (upper) Two-sided Both versions exist
Key use cases Theoretical proofs, base for others LLN, streaming algorithms Randomized algorithms, ML, hashing
Historical origin Andrey Markov, ~1884 Pafnuty Chebyshev, ~1867 Herman Chernoff, 1952

The Verdict

If your variables are independent, always use the Chernoff bound — its exponential tightness is dramatically superior. When independence fails, reach for Chebyshev if you know the variance. And when all else fails, Markov is always available as the universal safety net. Think of them as a hierarchy: each stronger tool requires more assumptions but pays you back with exponentially better probability guarantees.
